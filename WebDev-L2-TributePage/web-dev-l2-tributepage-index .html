A tribute to

Ada Lovelace

The woman who imagined what computers could become before a single one existed.

1815 – 1852

Her Story

Augusta Ada Byron was born in London on 10 December 1815, the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron and Anne Isabella Milbanke. Her parents separated within weeks of her birth, and Byron left England for good, so Ada never really knew her father. Her mother was determined that she would grow up disciplined and logical, and made sure she was taught mathematics and science from a young age.

In 1833, through the scientist Mary Somerville, Ada met the inventor Charles Babbage. He showed her a working section of his Difference Engine, a mechanical calculator, and she was captivated. The two became close friends and wrote to each other for years about mathematics and machines. Ada kept studying seriously, later with the mathematician Augustus De Morgan, and she married William King in 1835. When he was made Earl of Lovelace in 1838, she became the Countess of Lovelace.

Her most important work came in 1843. Babbage had designed a far more ambitious machine, the Analytical Engine, which was meant to be programmable but was never built. An Italian engineer, Luigi Menabrea, had written an article describing it, and Ada translated it into English. She then added her own Notes, which ended up much longer than the article itself. In the last of them, Note G, she laid out a step-by-step method for the machine to calculate Bernoulli numbers. Many people regard it as the first published computer program.

What set Ada apart was her imagination. She saw that a machine like this could work with any kind of symbol, not only numbers, and suggested it might even compose music. At the same time she was careful to say the engine could only do what people told it to do. Ada died in 1852 at the age of 36. Her ideas were largely forgotten, but as modern computers appeared in the twentieth century, people returned to her Notes and recognised how far ahead of her time she had been.

Key Moments

  1. 1815

    Born in London on 10 December.

  2. 1833

    Meets Charles Babbage and sees his Difference Engine. A lifelong friendship begins.

  3. 1835

    Marries William King, who becomes Earl of Lovelace in 1838.

  4. 1843

    Publishes her translation of Menabrea's article with her own Notes, including Note G.

  5. 1852

    Dies in London on 27 November, aged 36.

  6. 1950s

    Her Notes gain new attention as the first electronic computers arrive.

  7. 1980

    The programming language Ada is named in her honour.

  8. 2009

    Ada Lovelace Day begins, celebrating the achievements of women in science, technology, engineering and maths.

The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.

Ada Lovelace, Note G, 1843
